Special Participation E: Using ChatGPT to surface what you don't know

By Jermaine Lei 2025-12-08 GPT
View on Ed Stem →
For this assignment, I wanted to create a question generator of some kind. Instead of having the model explain the lecture to me, I flipped the direction and had it act as an “office hour simulator” where the model acts like a line of students asking questions about the lecture. The main idea was to surface the things I don’t know and wouldn’t think to ask on my own. Sometimes it’s really hard to pinpoint your own gaps in understanding so having the model generate realistic, conceptual, and mechanic-focused questions and even tweak baseline assumptions to test whether I truly understood the concepts felt like a unique way to self-check my knowledge. In order to make this work, I’ve created 2 prompts. The first one: The reason for adding the 3 different types of questions was to maximize the chances that the model would generate a conceptual question that hits an area of understanding that you may lack. After answering the questions in bullet point fashion, the second prompt generates its own “truth” response and compares it to yours: The main goal of this second prompt is to identify where your answer lacks some more concrete reasoning/information. This is helpful because even if the model identifies a missing piece of your response that you already know, seeing that missing piece connect to the rest of your answer can reinforce your understanding of the topic. In my example, I use the lecture 7 slides. The model’s student questions captured the tone and content of real confusion points. It asked about how the updates work, how the SVD pieces fit together, and how different assumptions would change the behavior of the algorithm. These kinds of questions immediately showed which parts of the lecture I understood well and which parts I was glossing over, and answering them myself first made those gaps obvious when the model later switched into instructor mode and filled in the missing steps. Overall, this turned out to be a genuinely useful learning tool. The model kept everything grounded in the lecture notes while still giving enough detail to strengthen my understanding, and its critiques helped sharpen my answers rather than just telling me they were wrong. Most importantly, this setup pushed me into a kind of deeper reasoning practice that is hard to create on my own because it required me to articulate ideas clearly, check my logic, and confront misunderstandings I did not realize I had. As a post-lecture study method, I would definitely use this approach again. Here is my annotated chat conversation using Lecture 7 as an example:

You are modeling a line of students waiting at office hours for this course.

Above I have shared lecture material such as slides, notes, or a worksheet. Use that as the main source for everything you do here.

Your task:

1. Read the material and identify the key ideas and skills it covers.

2. Based only on this material and basic background that a typical student in this class would already know, create seven realistic student questions.

3. The seven questions must follow this pattern:
- Two clarification questions about definitions, notation, or basic setup.
- Three why or how questions that dig into reasoning, assumptions, or connections between ideas.
- Two application questions that ask what would happen if we change an assumption, parameter, or example.

4. Make the questions sound like real questions a strong but slightly confused student might ask.

5. Make sure the questions span the full range of important ideas in the material.

6. Do not answer the questions. Number them from 1 to 7.

Now act as the instructor for this course.

Job 1:
Answer each of the seven questions in at most five sentences. Use the uploaded material as your main source. Add outside background only when absolutely necessary.

Job 2:
Compare your answer for each question with my answer in my previous message.
For each question:
- Say whether my answer is fully correct, partially correct, or incorrect.
- In two or three sentences explain what I did well and what I missed or got wrong.
- If I missed something important or said something inaccurate, provide a corrected or improved version.

Job 3:
Be explicit when going beyond the uploaded material.
Any time you use information that is not directly supported by the notes, begin the sentence with: EXTRA BACKGROUND.
If the material does not give enough information to answer a question confidently, say: Not answerable from the notes, and explain what additional information would be needed.

Stay grounded in the uploaded material, avoid guessing, and be honest about uncertainty.
