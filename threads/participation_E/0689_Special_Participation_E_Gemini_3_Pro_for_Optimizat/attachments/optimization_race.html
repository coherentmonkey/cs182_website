Optimization: The Math Behind the Magic

1. Why Momentum beats SGD

The Problem: In "ravines" (like this function), the surface is steep on the sides but flat along the bottom. SGD oscillates across the steep sides because the gradient ∇L is huge in the vertical direction.

vt = γ vt-1 - α ∇L(wt)

Understanding Beta/Gamma (γ): This parameter acts as "friction" or memory.
• High γ (0.9): The agent keeps 90% of its previous speed. It's like a heavy truck; it punches through small bumps but takes longer to turn.
• Low γ (0.5): The agent keeps only 50% of its speed. It's lighter and changes direction faster but gets stuck more easily.

The Result:
• Vertical Direction: Gradients flip signs (+, -, +, -). Summing them causes cancellation ($1 + (-1) = 0$). The oscillation dampens.
• Horizontal Direction: Gradients are small but consistent (+, +, +). Summing them builds velocity, accelerating down the valley.

2. Why Adam usually beats Momentum

The Problem: Momentum relies on a global Learning Rate (α). If the valley gets flatter, it slows down. If the gradient gets steeper, it can overshoot dangerously.

Step ≈ α · mt / √vt

Understanding Adam's Betas: Adam keeps two separate histories.
• β1 (Momentum): Tracks the direction (mean). Usually 0.9.
• β2 (Scaling): Tracks the magnitude/volatility (variance). Usually 0.999.
What changing β2 does: If you lower β2 (e.g., to 0.9), Adam forgets historical volatility faster. This makes it adapt to "new" terrain steepness very aggressively, which can sometimes be unstable.

The Math: Adam divides the momentum (mt) by the root mean square of gradients (√vt).
• Steep Slopes: Gradient is large → √vt is large → Step size decreases (Stabilizing).
• Flat Valleys: Gradient is tiny → √vt is tiny → Step size increases (Accelerating).
