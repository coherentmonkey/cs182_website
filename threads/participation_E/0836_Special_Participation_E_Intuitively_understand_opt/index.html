Special Participation E: Intuitively understand optimizer by Claude Sonnet 4.5

By Shuwei Yang 2025-12-11 Claude
View on Ed Stem →
I used Claude sonnet 4.5 as an interactive “pre/post-lecture reading” for Lecture 5 on optimizers (gradient descent, sign SGD, norm-based views, Shampoo, μP, and Muon). Instead of just asking it to summarize the slides, I treated Claude like a tutor I could program with prompts. I’ll attach the main prompts I used, plus a chunk of our conversation, so that other people can reuse or modify this setup if they want to self-study the same material. The first thing I asked for was a “100-year-old grandma” version of the lecture. I told Claude to imagine it was explaining optimizers to a very smart but non-technical grandma, starting from the foggy-mountain picture of gradient descent, then moving to sign SGD, different norms as different ways of constraining steps, and finally how Shampoo and μP/Muon fit into that story. I emphasized slow pacing, friendly tone, lots of analogies (water pipes for Xavier, rubber sheets and trampolines for norms and spectral stuff), and as few formulas as possible until the intuition felt solid. I also uploaded the Lecture 5 slides and kept asking it to tie things back to specific pages and symbols, so it wouldn’t drift into some generic “optimizers 101” description. Along the way it kept quizzing me with small questions like “why might sign SGD be useful?” or “why does it make sense to throw away Σ?” which forced me to answer in my own words instead of just passively reading. Even with that, there was one part of the lecture I still found very confusing: the chain from Xavier initialization → RMS norm → spectral norm → Shampoo. So I wrote a second, more focused prompt that asked Claude to explain only this chain, step by step, as a narrative. I asked it to start from the practical problem Xavier is trying to solve (“keep each layer’s activations at a reasonable scale”), then explain why that naturally leads to thinking in terms of “typical size per parameter” and the RMS norm, then reinterpret weight matrices as stretchy rubber sheets to motivate the spectral norm, and finally connect that back to the constrained optimization problem whose solution looks like a Shampoo update. This second pass helped a lot: once it framed RMS as “average size of an entry” and spectral norm as “maximum stretching factor in those units,” the ΔW ≈ −ηUVᵀ picture suddenly felt much less mysterious. I also tried to be critical, as the instructions ask. In my annotations on the trace, I point out places where the model is more intuitive than rigorous. For example, when it connects RMS norms to induced matrix norms and spectral norms, it sometimes glosses over details with “≈”, which is fine for building intuition but shouldn’t be mistaken for a proof. Its explanation of μP and Muon stays at a high level: it talks about reusing hyperparameters across model scales and 10× speedups in NanoGPT-style experiments, but it doesn’t really go into implementation details or computational costs. It also occasionally ignored my request to delay equations and introduced formulas earlier than I would have liked if this were a polished pre-lecture handout. These are places where I think a human reader needs to be cautious and treat the model as a helper, not an authority. Overall, though, this felt like a reasonable example of what special participation E is aiming for: instead of reading the slides in a passive way, I used an AI tool to build a small, reusable learning artifact for one lecture, and then interacted with it in a way that made me articulate my own understanding and push back when things were unclear. If other students want to try something similar, they could reuse my prompts on a different topic (attention, diffusion, etc.), put the model into this “grandma mode” to get a soft first pass, and then write their own follow-up prompts for the one or two conceptual chains they still don’t understand, making the model re-explain those pieces until they can say them back in their own words.
