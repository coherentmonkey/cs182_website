Conversation Link - https://chatgpt.com/share/6933c8cc-4f6c-8012-9651-4b391b6f512b I used ChatGPT (GPT-5.1 Thinking) to work through the non-coding parts of a CS182 homework (the questions I focused on were mostly about autoencoders/VAEs and ridge/self-attention). I interacted with it like a tutor: rather than just asking “what’s the answer to 3(b)?”, I had it re-teach concepts (encoder vs autoencoder vs VAE, Gaussians, randomness in the latent space, etc.), and then apply those ideas to the actual sub-questions. Executive summary Overall performance. Roughly speaking, the model could one-shot a majority of the subparts (I’d estimate ~60–70%) when the question was conceptual, algebraic, or about interpreting a loss function. When it struggled, it was usually on: subtle complexity analysis (missing or adding extra factors), unclear encoder/decoder notation in a complicated objective, or anything that required actually seeing a graph/figure. Strengths. Very good at re-explaining core concepts from the ground up (e.g., what a Gaussian is, what an encoder is, what a VAE is, and how noise injection works). Pretty good at mapping math to intuition (e.g., explaining where randomness enters a VAE and what it does, or what different terms in an ELBO correspond to). When I pushed it to slow down and justify each step, it usually corrected itself and converged to the right answer. Weaknesses / failure modes. It tends to be overconfident on complexity questions and sometimes adds an extra factor of n or d in the big-O expression. It occasionally blurred the roles of p and q in the VAE loss until I explicitly forced it to pin down “which one is the encoder, which one is the decoder.” It can’t read tiny graphs/plots from the PDF, so for at least one “pick the most U-shaped curve” style question, it had to rely entirely on my textual description rather than the actual image. Hallucinations. I didn’t catch any wild hallucinations like made-up theorems or algorithms, but I did see: Confident but slightly wrong complexity bounds (e.g., overcounting a matrix dimension). Slightly different forms of the loss than what we use in class (equivalent up to constants or sign conventions, but not exactly what appears in the homework). Overall, my takeaway is that GPT-5.1 is very capable as a conceptual tutor, and “pretty good but not fully trustworthy” as an answer-oracle. You need enough understanding to check its work and push back. Question-by-question behavior (high level) (You can adjust this to match your exact HW number / question labels.) Question 3 – Autoencoders, VAEs, and ELBO terms What I tested it on: Definitions of encoder, autoencoder, and variational autoencoder. Intuition behind Gaussians and the reparameterization trick. Interpreting the VAE loss (reconstruction + KL) and mapping pieces to encoder/decoder. A multiple-choice subpart involving a “most U-shaped” curve where I had to interpret the plot. Where it did well (one-shot or close): Gave a clear, layered explanation of: what an encoder does, what an autoencoder does, how a VAE differs, and why we add noise (sampling in latent space, regularization, smoothness). Re-taught Gaussians in simple terms and connected that to the Gaussian latent prior in the VAE. When I asked “where is this randomness/noise coming from and what does it do?”, it correctly pointed to the sampling from z∼qϕ​(z∣x) and explained that the reparameterization trick makes this differentiable. For the VAE objective, once prompted, it correctly identified that: qϕ​(z∣x) corresponds to the encoder, and pθ​(x∣z) corresponds to the decoder. Where it struggled / needed dragging: Given just the “complicated ass loss function,” it initially didn’t explicitly label which term belonged to encoder vs decoder—so I had to ask directly: “is it safe on a midterm to assume q is the decoder and p is the encoder, or what should I look for?” That pushed it to clarify that q is the encoder (approx posterior) and p is the decoder (likelihood). For the graph-based subpart (“most U-shaped is option B”), the model couldn’t see the graph properly. I ended up using its conceptual explanation of what the curve should look like, combined with my own visual inspection, to pick the correct option. So it helped, but it wasn’t independently solving that one. Question 4 – Ridge regression / self-attention-style math (non-coding parts only) What I used it for: Conceptual pieces around online/recursive updates, ridge-style penalties, and how these tie into efficient self-attention. Understanding how different terms in the loss or update equations affect computational cost and memory. Strengths: Gave good explanations of why we care about efficient updates and connected them to the idea of reusing previous computations instead of recomputing from scratch. When I asked it to “explain from the ground up” it was good at turning each step into something intuitive (e.g., “this part is like keeping a running summary; this part is like correcting your estimate with the new data point”). Weaknesses: On some of the big-O complexity questions, it tended to overshoot, e.g., something that could be done in O(nd2) it might initially describe as O(nd3) until I forced it to count operations more carefully. This is a recurring theme: it knows the right algorithmic idea, but it’s sloppy about exact asymptotics unless you police it. Questions 5 and 7 – Additional conceptual / non-coding parts Here I mainly used it as a concept explainer and sanity-check, not just a direct answer machine. It gave reasonable answers on first pass, but the real value was in: rephrasing the question, highlighting which quantities matter (e.g., what’s being regularized, what’s being predicted), and giving analogies that made the math feel less abstract. At the end, I also asked it to evaluate my level of preparedness for the final based on my questions. That response was more “vibe-check” than science, but it was helpful for me to see what it thought my weak spots were (mostly: being shaky at mapping formulas to pictures and at carefully tracking complexity). Strategies I used to steer the model In the annotated log I’m attaching, I call out some of the strategies I used: Force “from-first-principles” explanations. Instead of just “what’s the answer to 3(b)?”, I first asked it to re-teach encoders/autoencoders/VAEs and Gaussians “from the ground up”. This made it expose assumptions and definitions I could later check. Ask targeted follow-ups when something feels hand-wavy. Example: “Where is this randomness/noise coming in from? What does it do?” and “How do we tell from that complicated loss which is the encoder vs decoder?” These forced it to pin down the role of each term instead of staying vague. Use it to reason about shapes/curves, then apply my own visual check. For the “most U-shaped” graph, it couldn’t see the figure, so I had it describe what shape we should expect, then I matched that to the multiple-choice options. Push back on complexity claims. When it gave a complexity I didn’t trust, I asked it to explicitly count matrix-vector vs matrix-matrix ops and justify the O(⋅). This usually surfaced the mistake and got it to correct itself. Takeaway For this homework, GPT-5.1 Thinking was not a magic “give me the solution key” button, but it was a strong tutor for the non-coding parts: It can one-shot many conceptual subparts, It sometimes fumbles details, especially asymptotic complexity, And it absolutely requires an engaged human who’s willing to question it, check it against the official solutions, and drag it when it’s confident but wrong.
