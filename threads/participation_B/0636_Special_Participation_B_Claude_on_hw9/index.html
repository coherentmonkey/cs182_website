Link to conversation: https://claude.ai/share/00a345bb-cd9c-41c1-879c-09aeda6818f9 Although the special participation B is about coding, the particular notebook I worked with (Visualizing_BERT.ipynb) was essentially non-coding. All the interesting work was in interpreting attention visualizations and using those interpretations to feed an LLM, not in writing code. Strategy I started naively by asking the model to “read” one of the BertViz attention diagrams directly from an image. This immediately exposed a major issue. The model’s vision was extremely unreliable. It confidently described patterns that did not match what I saw in the notebook. For example, it claimed that the word “dog” had the strongest attention to “dog” on the right, while the actual strongest attention was to “The”. In other words, it was hallucinating a plausible attention map rather than faithfully reporting what was in the picture. Which is understandable, since LLMs’ ability to interpret images is not that good. After this first attempt, I explicitly told it to forget its own image reading and decided I could not treat its visual perception as trustworthy. From that point on, I switched to a different interaction pattern: I carefully read each visualization myself and converted it into a precise textual description: tokens, layers, heads, what the patterns were, etc. The LLM acted as the reasoning engine. Given my textual description, it answered the questions about GPT-2 and BERT attention. This setup had an obvious flaw. How well the model did hinged on my ability to accurately describe the visualization. When I forgot to describe the stronger connection between the same words in cell 8, obviously the model could not include that in its answers, since the information was not given. There was also a practical bottleneck. Describing all available information was too difficult. There are multiple layers and multiple heads to choose from, plus expanded views of queries and keys. To keep the interaction manageable, I deliberately ignored the vector views and focused only on the attention lines, since most of the problems could be answered from the line patterns alone. For the final sub-questions in parts (c) and (d), I made a deliberate choice not to describe the visualizations at all. Those figures would have been extremely cumbersome to encode in text, and the questions themselves were fundamentally conceptual (e.g., “what tokens would you expect strong attention between?”). In that setting, I felt that an inaccurate human description might do more harm than good. Instead, I only gave the model the textual setup from the notebook and asked it to reason from its general understanding of attention, rather than from any specific picture. Evaluation Once the model had a clean textual description, its answers were often rich and well-structured. However, I’m not sure how much of this good result should be attributed to very specific descriptions of visualizations. In this setting, the main issue was mis-emphasis. For example, in the GPT-2 part, it initially tried to attribute deep linguistic significance to the word “The”, and I had to push it to reinterpret that as a computational significance of being in position 0, rather than the determiner’s semantics. The behavior changed slightly on the last two questions, where I did not describe any visualization at all and simply gave it the textual setup. There, the model leaned purely on its prior knowledge of how attention should behave. It immediately highlighted words that have similar meanings, pairs like “happy” vs. “sad” and “I” vs. “I”, and so on, and gave a pretty plausible story about which attention weights would receive large gradients. But it did not incorporate patterns in previous answers in its answer without a direct prompt, such as the special tokens ([CLS] and [SEP]). In other words, when the question was conceptual, its answers were incomplete but not incorrect, even without any grounding in a specific figure. Of course, in these cases, there is no guarantee that its answers match the actual visualization in the notebook. It’s giving a good generic answer, not a picture-specific one. Synthesis Overall, this interaction ended up looking less like “code co-pilot” and more like a division of labor between human perception and LLM reasoning. The LLM was very good at interpreting structured textual summaries of attention patterns, and very bad at extracting those patterns directly from images, which is exactly what you would expect, given how much more mature its language abilities are than its vision. If you let it stare at the picture by itself, it will hallucinate a plausible story. If you constrain it to human descriptions, it becomes a pretty decent analysis assistant that can map those descriptions onto concepts. And for high-level conceptual questions, it can sometimes ignore the visualization entirely and rely on its internal model of how attention should behave, as long as you are comfortable with the fact that those answers are not grounded in the specific figure. In that sense, this “non-coding coding homework” shows that current LLMs are much more reliable as text-based reasoning engines, and that effective human-model cooperation is very important.
